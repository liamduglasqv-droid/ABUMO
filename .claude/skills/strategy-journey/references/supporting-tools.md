# Supporting best-practice tools

The book gathers established frameworks and plugs each into one of the five models. Use them for evidence inside a path step, not as separate exercises. Each entry says what the tool is, where it plugs in, and how to use it quickly.

## Mission Model tools

**SWOT** (internal Strengths and Weaknesses; external Opportunities and Threats)
Plugs into Path 1, step 2. Turn it into actions with the TOWS moves: SO (use strengths to capture opportunities), WO (fix weaknesses to capture opportunities), ST (use strengths to counter threats), WT (defend). Tie each item to an operating-model component so it can be acted on.

**PESTEL** (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal)
Plugs into Path 1, step 1. For each factor record the trend, its impact on the client (H/M/L), the timing and the response. Treat regulation as a way to play a new game: a new rule can support a compliant USP.

**Game Plan / competitor analysis**
List the competitors and the likely **disrupters**: non-competitors from adjacent industries, platforms. For each strategic option, write down the pros, cons, cost, risk and time to value, then choose explicitly. A strategy without a game plan fails (see Tutorspree). For a deeper industry view, see the `competitor-analysis` skill.

**Mission and vision "pull" test**
Would customers, talent and partners come to you because of this statement? Does it make some choices easier to refuse?

## Business Model tools

**Business Model Canvas (BMC; Osterwalder, Strategyzer)**
Nine blocks: Customer Segments, Value Propositions, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams, Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partners, Cost Structure. The Digital Business Blueprint builds on it. Use the BMC to capture the *current* model quickly.

**Lean Canvas (Maurya)**
Problem, Customer Segments, Unique Value Proposition, Solution, Channels, Revenue Streams, Cost Structure, Key Metrics, Unfair Advantage. Best for start-ups and new ventures, because it is assumption-heavy.

**Value Proposition Canvas (VPC)**
Customer profile (Jobs, Pains, Gains) matched against the value map (Products & Services, Pain Relievers, Gain Creators). Aim for a clear fit on the most important jobs. Feeds row 1 (Goals) of the Customer Journey Map.

**Customer Avatar**
A named persona covering demographics, goals and values, challenges and pains, where they spend time (touchpoints), how they buy, objections, and what they see as success. Build it from real data and interviews.

**Innovation Adoption Lifecycle (Rogers) and Crossing the Chasm (Moore)**
Segments: Innovators, Early Adopters | *chasm* | Early Majority, Late Majority, Laggards. Many start-ups fail to get from early adopters to the early majority. In the digital economy the curve shifts left (hyperadoption, Big Bang Disruption), so trial users and "everybody else" can arrive very quickly. Use it to decide when to scale. WebVan scaled too early.

**Digital business model types**
Pipeline or product; service or solution (e.g. IBM Business Solutions); platform or multi-sided marketplace (B2C, B2B, B2B2C, P2P); subscription; freemium; information services and data products (create once, sell many times, unlimited inventory); ecosystem orchestrator. For each, check the network effects, the data assets it builds and the defensibility.

**Pricing and transaction models**
One-off, subscription, usage-based, commission or take rate, advertising, licensing, outcome-based. Check the price elasticity: services are usually less price-sensitive than products.

## Value Model tools

**Ansoff Matrix:** market penetration (existing product, existing market), product development, market development and diversification. Diversification is the riskiest move. Lego over-diversified into clothing, theme parks and media without the capabilities to support it.

**Service Innovation Matrix (SIM)**
Map service innovation strategies to tactics: improve an existing service, extend it along the journey, bundle with partners, or build a new service in a new journey. Rank the opportunities from the Customer Journey Map by type and risk. The book's own grid isn't in the preview, so present this as an adaptation.

**Value disciplines (Treacy & Wiersema)**
Product Leadership, Customer Intimacy, Operational Excellence. The traditional advice is to lead in one and be at parity in the others. The Strategy Journey says digital leaders capture value across all three and more. Use it to position the client, then challenge that position.

**Porter's Value Chain**
Primary activities: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service. Support activities: firm infrastructure, HR, technology, procurement. Use it to find the client's position in its industry value chain. Reuse industry reference value chains to speed up capability modelling.

**Value streams**
End-to-end flows from a trigger to a stakeholder outcome, e.g. "Acquire customer" or "Fulfil order". They connect the customer journey (outside-in) to capabilities (inside-out). See `process-redesign` for Lean waste analysis of a stream.

**Value ecosystem roles**
For each player in the ecosystem (customers, suppliers, partners, platforms, regulators, communities, competitors or co-opetitors), map the value they give and receive. Then find capability gaps that a partner could fill instead of building them in-house.

## Operating Model tools

**Operating Model Canvas (Campbell, Gutierrez & Lancelott), POLISM**
**P**rocesses (the value chain), **O**rganisation (people and structure), **L**ocations, **I**nformation (systems and data), **S**uppliers (partners), **M**anagement system (planning, budgets, performance, governance). The value proposition sits at the centre. Use it to capture the current enterprise-wide operating model. Then use the Service Operating Model Canvas for each value stream.

**Capability modelling**
A capability is *what* the business does, independent of how or who does it: for example "Customer onboarding" or "Credit decisioning". Each capability combines people, process, data, technology and funding. Build a capability map with 2–3 levels. Tag USP (differentiating) vs commodity capabilities: invest in USP and standardise or outsource the commodity ones. Rate maturity from 1 to 5 (see `framework.md` §8) and heat-map the gaps.

**Zachman Framework**
A 6×6 grid: interrogatives (What, How, Where, Who, When, Why) against perspectives (planner, owner, designer, builder, implementer, operator). Use it as a completeness checklist for business architecture. Have all six questions been answered at each level?

**Architecture layers**
Business layer (mission, business model, value, capabilities), then information and data, then applications, then technology and infrastructure. Keep changes traceable from the top layer down.

## Transformation Model tools

**RACI** (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed)
Exactly one A per activity or decision. Use it for initiatives, capabilities and design decisions. It makes accountability clear and prevents hero behaviour.

**Growth vs fixed mindset (Dweck)**
A fixed mindset avoids challenge and sees failure as proof of limits. A growth mindset sees effort and failure as learning. To influence it: celebrate learning from contained experiments, model curiosity as a leader, give feedback on process rather than talent, and keep failure safe through prototyping.

**Governance**
- A **design authority** keeps designs aligned with the target operating model and architecture principles.
- The **change development lifecycle** runs idea → discovery → design → build → test → deploy → learn.
- **Transformation viewpoints** give each stakeholder the view they need: executive, business, change, technology, operations.

Avoid both extremes: bureaucracy that stifles agility, and no governance at all (silos and scope creep).

**Prioritisation**
Use a value vs effort matrix, weighted scoring against the six value measures, cost of delay, or MoSCoW. Separate mandatory (non-discretionary: compliance, maintenance) funding from discretionary (growth, innovation) funding.
